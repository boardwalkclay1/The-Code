# GITHUB LESSON 27 — ISSUES

=== PART 1 — [Issues](ca://s?q=Explain_GitHub_Issues) ===
Issues are notes used to track tasks, ideas, or problems.  
They help you organize work.  
They are useful for both solo and team projects.

=== PART 2 — [Creating an Issue](ca://s?q=Create_an_issue) ===
You can create an issue from the Issues tab.  
You give it a title and description.  
This defines what needs attention.

=== PART 3 — [Labels](ca://s?q=Issue_labels) ===
Labels categorize issues.  
Examples include “bug,” “feature,” or “help wanted.”  
Labels make issues easier to sort.

=== PART 4 — [Assignees](ca://s?q=Issue_assignees) ===
You can assign issues to specific people.  
This shows who is responsible.  
It keeps work organized.

=== PART 5 — [Comments](ca://s?q=Issue_comments) ===
Issues allow comments for discussion.  
People can ask questions or give updates.  
This keeps communication clear.

=== PART 6 — [Closing Issues](ca://s?q=Close_issue) ===
When an issue is solved, you close it.  
Closing marks the task as complete.  
It keeps your list clean.

=== PART 7 — [Reopening Issues](ca://s?q=Reopen_issue) ===
You can reopen issues if the problem returns.  
This keeps the history intact.  
It avoids creating duplicates.

=== PART 8 — [Issue Templates](ca://s?q=Issue_templates) ===
Templates help standardize issue creation.  
They guide users to provide the right information.  
They improve consistency.

=== PART 9 — [Milestones](ca://s?q=Issue_milestones) ===
Milestones group issues into goals.  
They help track progress toward larger tasks.  
They are useful for planning.

=== PART 10 — [Search](ca://s?q=Search_issues) ===
You can search issues by keyword.  
This helps you find related tasks.  
It keeps your workflow efficient.

=== PART 11 — [Filters](ca://s?q=Filter_issues) ===
Filters let you view issues by label, assignee, or status.  
This helps you focus on specific work.  
It keeps your list manageable.

=== PART 12 — [Issue Linking](ca://s?q=Link_issues) ===
You can link issues to each other.  
This shows relationships between tasks.  
It helps track dependencies.

=== PART 13 — [Issue References](ca://s?q=Reference_issue) ===
You can reference issues in commits or pull requests.  
This connects work to tasks.  
It improves traceability.

=== PART 14 — [Issue Notifications](ca://s?q=Issue_notifications) ===
GitHub notifies you when issues change.  
This keeps you updated.  
It helps you stay involved.

=== PART 15 — [Public Issues](ca://s?q=Public_issues) ===
Public repositories have public issues.  
Anyone can read them.  
This encourages collaboration.

=== PART 16 — [Private Issues](ca://s?q=Private_issues) ===
Private repositories restrict issue access.  
Only authorized users can see them.  
This protects sensitive work.

=== PART 17 — [Issue History](ca://s?q=Issue_history) ===
Issues keep a full history of comments and changes.  
This preserves context.  
It helps future contributors understand decisions.

=== PART 18 — [Why Issues Matter](ca://s?q=Why_Issues_matter) ===
Issues keep work organized.  
They help track progress.  
They improve communication.

=== PART 19 — [Issues for Solo Work](ca://s?q=Issues_for_solo_projects) ===
Even solo creators benefit from issues.  
They act as reminders and planning tools.  
They keep your project structured.

=== PART 20 — [Next Lesson](ca://s?q=Start_Lesson_28) ===
Next lesson explains Pull Requests.  
Pull Requests help you review and merge work.  
They are essential for collaboration.
